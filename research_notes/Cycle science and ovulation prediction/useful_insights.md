# Most useful, evidence-based insights from cycle tracking

Research note. Method caveat: the research proxy blocked direct fetches of acog.org, nhs.uk, nice.org.uk and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. The findings below come from search-result extracts of those pages, plus secondary clinical sources (MSD/Merck Manual, GPnotebook, NHS 111 Wales, PubMed abstracts). Exact guideline wording should be checked against the primary pages before it is quoted in a product.

## Proposed ranking: highest-value insights and the minimum data each needs

Ranking criteria: (a) strength of evidence and guideline backing, (b) whether it changes what the user does (see a GP, take a test, time intercourse), (c) whether it can be derived from minimal data.

| Rank | Insight | Minimum data | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Next period prediction with an honest uncertainty range (the top reason people track) | Period start dates (3 or more cycles) | Strong for user value (Epstein 2017). Accuracy is limited by natural variability (Bull 2019) |
| 2 | "Worth seeing a GP" flags for cycle length and regularity: frequent (<24 d, or <21 d in ACOG/NHS terms), infrequent (>38 d, or >35 d), shortest-to-longest variation >7–9 d, no period for 3 months or more | Period start dates | Strong: FIGO 2018, ACOG 651, NHS |
| 3 | Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) flags: period >7–8 days, changing products every 1–2 h, clots >2.5 cm, flooding through to clothes or bedding, double protection, tiredness or breathlessness (possible anaemia) | Period start and end dates + daily flow, ideally with HMB markers (hourly changes, clots, flooding) and impact on daily life | Strong: NHS, NICE NG88, FIGO |
| 4 | Bleeding between periods or after sex (always a reason to see a GP) | A "spotting/bleeding outside period" log | Strong: NHS, FIGO (intermenstrual bleeding is an AUB category) |
| 5 | A shareable cycle summary for clinicians: cycle lengths, durations, flow, flags | Same as above | Strong user value (Epstein 2017: informing conversations with healthcare providers). Guidelines rely on this history |
| 6 | TTC support: a broad fertile window, intercourse every 2–3 days, when to test, when to seek help (1 year, or earlier if aged 36 or over) | Period start dates + age. LH or BBT only if the user wants narrower windows | Strong for the guidance (NICE CG156, NHS). Weak for calendar-only ovulation prediction (Wilcox 2000) |
| 7 | Perimenopause signal: a persistent ≥7-day difference between consecutive cycle lengths (STRAW+10 early transition), and ≥60 days without a period (late transition) | Period start dates + age (roughly 40 and over) | Moderate to strong (STRAW+10 consensus staging) |
| 8 | Symptom-to-phase patterns that the user confirms prospectively: PMS/PMDD (2 or more cycles of daily ratings), menstrual migraine (2 of 3 cycles, day −2 to +3) | Period dates + daily symptom ratings | Strong as a diagnostic aid (DSM-5 requires prospective ratings; ICHD-3 recommends diaries) |
| 9 | Personal trends: cycle length drifting over time, mean ± SD | Period start dates over 6 to 12 months or more | Moderate (Bull 2019 population data on age-related shortening) |
| Low / avoid | "Cycle syncing" of diet, workouts or productivity to phases | n/a | Weak. Exercise-performance meta-analysis found only trivial, inconsistent effects |

## The menstrual cycle as a "vital sign": which changes warrant seeing a doctor?

### Takeaway
ACOG (US, adolescents), FIGO (international, reproductive-age adults) and NHS (UK public) give closely aligned red flags. The main ones are cycles that are too short or too long, irregular cycles, periods lasting more than 7–8 days, heavy-flow markers, an absent period, and any bleeding between periods or after sex. Nearly all of these can be detected from period dates, flow and a spotting log.

### Cited Findings
- ACOG Committee Opinion No. 651, "Menstruation in Girls and Adolescents: Using the Menstrual Cycle as a Vital Sign" (Obstet Gynecol, Dec 2015, 126(6):e143–e146), argues that evaluating the menstrual cycle as a vital sign "reinforce[s] its importance in assessing overall health status", and that spotting abnormal patterns early may help identify adult health concerns — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26595586/)
- ACOG 651: by the 3rd year after menarche, cycles are typically 21–45 days. An adolescent should be evaluated if cycles are >45 or <21 days apart, bleeding lasts >7 days, there is a single gap of 3 months between bleeds, or products are changed more often than every 1–2 hours — [Children's Mercy summary of ACOG 651](https://www.childrensmercy.org/health-care-providers/refer-or-manage-a-patient/connect-with-childrens-mercy/newsletter-the-link/link-newsletter-september-202322/wise-use/); [Contemporary Pediatrics](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/menstruation-sixth-vital-sign)
- Causes of abnormal bleeding listed in ACOG 651 include hyperandrogenic anovulation (for example PCOS), coagulopathy, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, hypothalamic dysfunction, primary ovarian insufficiency, medications, STIs and uterine lesions — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26595586/)
- FIGO AUB System 1 (Munro et al. 2018 revision), normal limits for adults: frequency 24–38 days (<24 is "frequent", >38 is "infrequent"); regularity, defined as the shortest-to-longest cycle variation, normal at ≤7–9 days; duration ≤8 days. Intermenstrual bleeding is classed as abnormal — [MSD Manual: Normal Menstrual Parameters](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/normal-menstrual-parameters); [Munro 2018, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30198563/); [FIGO System 1 overview, Thieme](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0045-1815710)
- Conflict note: older FIGO wording (2011) allowed 2–20 days of variation over 12 months as "regular", and some summaries still cite it. The 2018 revision tightened this to ≤7–9 days (age-dependent) — [Thieme overview](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/s-0045-1815710) vs [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/normal-menstrual-parameters)
- NHS: see a GP if heavy periods are affecting your life, you have had heavy periods for some time, you have severe period pain, you bleed between periods or after sex, or heavy periods come with other symptoms such as pain when peeing, pooing or having sex. Rarely, heavy periods can be a sign of womb cancer — [NHS Heavy periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/) (via search extract); [NHS 111 Wales](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/heavyperiods,menorrhagia/)

### Inferences
- For a UK adult user, a sensible flag set is: cycles <21 or >35–38 days repeatedly, shortest-to-longest variation >7–9 days across recent cycles, bleeding >7–8 days, no period for 3 months or more (when not pregnant, on hormonal contraception or breastfeeding), any intermenstrual or post-coital bleeding, and HMB markers. The <21 / >35 thresholds in the brief are common NHS and clinical shorthand. FIGO's 24/38 is the formal adult definition. An app could use FIGO for "outside typical range" and require persistence over 2–3 cycles before prompting a GP visit, to avoid false alarms.
- Flags should be framed as "worth discussing with a GP", not as a diagnosis.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch the primary NHS "Irregular periods" page to quote its exact thresholds (nhs.uk was blocked). The figure that NHS says normal cycles are 21–35 days is from memory and was not verified this session.
- I could not access the full ACOG 651 text table directly; the thresholds above come from secondary summaries.

## What can be derived from period dates + flow alone?

### Takeaway
Start dates alone give cycle length, its trend, variability (shortest to longest, SD), missed periods and perimenopause signals. Adding end dates gives period duration. Adding daily flow, with simple HMB markers (hourly changes, clots ≥10p coin, flooding, double protection), gives the guideline-backed signs of heavy menstrual bleeding and anaemia risk. Flow intensity alone (light/medium/heavy) is subjective. The concrete NHS markers are more actionable.

### Cited Findings
- NHS signs of heavy periods: changing a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours (or emptying a cup more often than recommended), needing 2 types of product together, periods lasting more than 7 days, clots larger than about 2.5 cm (a 10p coin), bleeding through to clothes or bedding, avoiding daily activities, feeling tired or short of breath a lot — [NHS Heavy periods (search extract)](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/); [NHS 111 Wales](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/heavyperiods,menorrhagia/)
- NICE NG88 defines HMB by impact on quality of life ("excessive menstrual blood loss which interferes with a woman's physical, social, emotional and/or material quality of life"), not by measured volume. Treatment aims to improve quality of life rather than reduce blood loss as such — [NICE NG88 PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/resources/heavy-menstrualbleeding-assessment-and-management-pdf-1837701412549); [Practice Nurse summary](https://practicenurse.co.uk/guidelines/heavy-menstrual-bleeding-assessment-and-management)
- The objective research definition is >80 mL per cycle (average 30–40 mL). 80 mL was chosen as roughly the most a woman on a normal diet can lose without becoming iron deficient — [GPnotebook: Menorrhagia](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/gynaecology/menorrhagia)
- NICE has updated NG88 to remove the old recommendation against routine serum ferritin testing in HMB, citing the risk of iron deficiency in this group — [NICE NG88 update information](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Update-information). (The search summary dated this change July 2026. I could not open the page to confirm the date.)
- STRAW+10: the early menopausal transition starts with increased variability, defined as a persistent difference of 7 days or more between consecutive cycle lengths. This begins on average 6–8 years before the final menstrual period. 12–25% of women see little or no cycle change before menopause — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/213402); [Harlow et al. STRAW+10, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3232023/)
- STRAW+10 defines "persistent" as recurring within 10 cycles of the first variable cycle. The late transition is marked by ≥60 days without a period — [Merck Manual: Stages of Menopause](https://merckmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/stages-of-menopause) (thresholds as I recall the STRAW+10 table; I did not open the page to confirm the wording)
- Real-world data from 612,613 ovulatory cycles (124,648 Natural Cycles users): mean cycle 29.3 d; mean follicular phase 16.9 d (95% CI 10–30); mean luteal phase 12.4 d (7–17). Mean cycle length fell by 0.18 d per year of age from 25 to 45, almost entirely through a shorter follicular phase. Within-woman variation was 14% higher with BMI >35 — [Bull et al. 2019, npj Digital Medicine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6710244/); [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2019/aug/individualised-approach-fertile-windows-could-benefit-many-women)

### Inferences
- Minimum data model: start date (required), end date (strongly recommended), daily flow with optional HMB markers (product changes ≤2 h, clot ≥10p, flooding, double protection), and an "impact on day" toggle that mirrors NICE's quality-of-life definition. A "spotting outside period" log is needed for the intermenstrual-bleeding flag.
- Heavy periods plus fatigue or breathlessness is a reasonable "ask your GP about a blood test (FBC/ferritin)" prompt, consistent with the NHS symptom list and NICE's move away from discouraging ferritin tests.
- Perimenopause detection is only meaningful for users aged roughly 40 and over, and it needs about 10 or more logged cycles to judge "persistence". Natural age-related shortening (Bull 2019) should not be mistaken for the transition.
- Prediction intervals should be personal (based on the user's own SD), not a fixed 28-day assumption, given the wide follicular-phase range.

### Gaps
- I found no validated algorithm for flagging anaemia risk from app flow logs. The link to anaemia rests on guideline symptom lists, not on app-validated scoring.
- PBAC (pictorial blood loss chart) is a validated volume proxy used in research, but I did not source it this session.

## Symptom-phase correlations: what has evidence vs "cycle syncing"

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence-based symptom use is prospective daily tracking, which is how PMDD (DSM-5, 2 or more cycles) and menstrual migraine (ICHD-3, attacks on day −2 to +3 in 2 of 3 cycles) are actually diagnosed. Retrospective recall of premenstrual symptoms is unreliable, so a tracker adds real value here. Population-level claims that each phase suits particular foods, workouts or work tasks ("cycle syncing") are not supported. The best exercise meta-analysis found only a trivial early-follicular dip and recommends personal, not generic, guidance.

### Cited Findings
- DSM-5 PMDD requires symptoms in most cycles of the past year, confirmed by at least two cycles of prospective daily ratings, because retrospective reports have poor validity — [Eisenlohr-Moul et al. 2017, C-PASS, Am J Psychiatry](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510); [PMC5205545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5205545)
- The DRSP (Daily Record of Severity of Problems) is the standard daily rating tool. C-PASS scoring of 2 or more cycles of DRSP agreed with expert diagnosis about 98% of the time. Retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predict a prospective diagnosis — [PMC5205545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5205545); [IAPMD FAQ on DRSP](https://faq.iapmd.org/en/articles/8232981-what-is-drsp-and-why-is-it-important-for-pmdd-diagnosis-and-research)
- Premenstrual exacerbation (PME) of existing mood disorders is a recognised, separate pattern. Tracking can help tell PME apart from PMDD — [Premenstrual Exacerbations of Mood Disorders, PMC8502143](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8502143/); [PMC11338788](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11338788/)
- ICHD-3: menstrual migraine is attacks starting on day 1 ± 2 (days −2 to +3) of menstruation in at least 2 of 3 cycles. "Pure" means attacks only in that window; "menstrually related" means attacks at other times too. A prospective diary over 3 cycles is recommended for confirmation (required for research, not for clinical diagnosis). There is no day 0 — [ICHD-3 A1.1.1](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-1-migraine-without-aura/a1-1-1-pure-menstrual-migraine-without-aura/); [PMC9535967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9535967)
- McNulty et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50(10):1813–1827), a systematic review and meta-analysis: exercise performance "might be trivially reduced during the early follicular phase". Because of the trivial effect size, large between-study variation and poor study quality, "general guidelines on exercise performance across the menstrual cycle cannot be formed". A personalised approach is recommended — [RRM Academy abstract](https://rrmacademy.org/library/the-effects-of-menstrual-cycle-phase-on-exercise-performance-in-eumenorrheic-wom-ouqt6anh/); [Northumbria repository](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/43759)

### Inferences
- Strong use case: "Your logged headaches cluster on day −2 to +3 in 3 of the last 3 cycles. This pattern is worth mentioning to your GP." Similarly, "low mood consistently in the 7 days before your period and clearing within a few days of it starting" for PMS/PMDD, with the DRSP as the model for the rating format.
- Insights should be personal and only shown once 2–3 cycles of prospective data exist. Phase-based advice should not be pushed from population averages.
- Sleep and energy: plausible luteal-phase effects exist, but I did not find guideline-level evidence this session. Treat these as "show the user their own pattern" only.

### Gaps
- I could not locate a reliable systematic review of mood across the cycle in the general (non-PMDD) population. Schmalenberger's methods work turned up, but not a review with that finding.
- I found no peer-reviewed evidence base for diet or "seed cycling" claims. Absence of evidence was not formally sourced.
- Sleep-phase evidence was not sourced.

## Insights for trying to conceive (TTC)

### Takeaway
The highest-value, best-evidenced TTC guidance is simple. Have intercourse every 2–3 days without timing it to the day; test from the first day of a missed period (or 21 days after unprotected sex if cycles are unknown); seek help after 1 year of trying, or sooner if aged 36 or over or if there is a known risk factor. Calendar-only "ovulation day" predictions are unreliable: the fertile window varies widely even in regular cycles. Apps should show a broad window and say how uncertain it is.

### Cited Findings
- NICE CG156: people who have not conceived after 1 year of regular unprotected intercourse (every 2–3 days) should be offered clinical assessment. Intercourse every 2–3 days optimises the chance of pregnancy — [NICE CG156 (PDF copy)](https://drseckin.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/cg156-assessment-and-treatment-for-people-with-fertility-problems-old.pdf); [NICE CG156 patient info](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg156/ifp/chapter/How-long-does-it-take-to-get-pregnant)
- Earlier referral is recommended for women aged 36 or over, or with a known cause or predisposing factor for infertility — [CG156 PDF](https://drseckin.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/cg156-assessment-and-treatment-for-people-with-fertility-problems-old.pdf); [Brainscape summary of CG156](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/nice-guidance-cg156-fertility-treatment-10996690/packs/18860504)
- CG156: a woman with regular cycles is likely to be ovulating. Ovulation is confirmed with a mid-luteal progesterone test about 7 days before the expected period (day 21 of a 28-day cycle, day 28 of a 35-day cycle) — [GPnotebook: day-21 progesterone](https://gpnotebook.com/pages/gynaecology/day-21-progesterone-for-ovulation); [CG156 PDF](https://drseckin.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/cg156-assessment-and-treatment-for-people-with-fertility-problems-old.pdf)
- My understanding, not verified this session, is that CG156 also advises against using BBT charts to confirm ovulation, and that timed intercourse with ovulation kits can add stress. The search summary said CG156 has no specific recommendation on ovulation kits — [CG156 PDF](https://drseckin.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/cg156-assessment-and-treatment-for-people-with-fertility-problems-old.pdf)
- NHS: pregnancy tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period. If you don't know when your period is due, test at least 21 days after unprotected sex. A negative result is less reliable, so retest after a few days and see a GP if the period still has not come — [NHS: Doing a pregnancy test (search extract)](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/trying-for-a-baby/doing-a-pregnancy-test/); [NHS 111 Wales](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/LiveWell/Pregnancy/WhenTest)
- Wilcox et al. 2000 (BMJ 321:1259; 221 women, 696 cycles, ovulation dated by urinary hormones): women had at least a 10% chance of being in their fertile window on every day from day 6 to day 21. Only about 30% had their fertile window entirely within days 10–17. Women should be told the fertile window "can be highly unpredictable, even if their cycles are usually regular" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11082086/); [PMC27529](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC27529)
- Bull 2019: the follicular phase ranged widely (95% CI 10–30 days), while the luteal phase was more stable (7–17 days). Most cycle-length variation therefore comes before ovulation, which makes forward prediction of ovulation from the period start hard — [Bull 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6710244/)
- App accuracy: reviews report a sensitivity of 35–94% for predicting the fertile window, and calendar apps cannot pinpoint the day of ovulation. About 79% of fertility apps and 75% of websites define the fertile window inaccurately (secondary sources) — [Pacific Fertility Center blog](https://www.pacificfertilitycenter.com/blog/fertility-apps-and-websites-are-they-accurate); [JSR pilot study](https://jsr.org/index.php/path/article/view/1216)

### Inferences
- Minimum data for TTC: period start dates + age + "TTC since" date. That is enough to show a broad window (defensibly about 6 days ending on the estimated ovulation day, widened by the user's own variability), the message "every 2–3 days is enough", a "test from [expected period date]" prompt, and a "you've been trying 12 months (or 6 months if 36 or over, as a cautious prompt) — see your GP" milestone.
- On the 6-month figure: NICE says "earlier referral" for 36+ but, in the extracts I saw, gives no specific number of months. "6 months" comes from other bodies (for example ASRM for 35+). Present it as "speak to your GP sooner", not as a NICE-specified 6 months.
- A day-21 progesterone test should be timed to the user's own cycle length (7 days before the expected period). Telling users this is a concrete, useful derived insight.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact CG156 wording on BBT charts and ovulation kits because nice.org.uk was blocked.
- The "79% of apps" figure comes from a secondary blog. I did not trace the primary paper (believed to be Duane et al. 2016, J Am Board Fam Med).

## What users actually find valuable vs noise

### Takeaway
The main motivations for tracking are remembering and predicting the next period and having data to bring to healthcare conversations. Inaccurate predictions destroy trust, and apps that assume one life stage or one user identity alienate people. Accuracy, transparency, clinician-ready summaries and life-stage flexibility matter more than gamified or pink "insights".

### Cited Findings
- Epstein et al. 2017, CHI ("Examining Menstrual Tracking to Inform the Design of Personal Informatics Tools", Best Paper Award): based on about 2,000 app reviews, a survey of 687 people and 12 follow-up interviews — [Paper PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2017-menstrualtracking.pdf); [ACM DOI 10.1145/3025453.3025635](https://www.springerprofessional.de/doi/10.1145/3025453.3025635)
- People track to remember and predict their period, and to inform conversations with healthcare providers. Six tracking methods were identified, including apps, awareness of premenstrual body signs, and simply remembering — [UW News](https://washington.edu/news/2017/05/02/period-tracking-apps-failing-users-in-basic-ways-study-finds); [Paper](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2017-menstrualtracking.pdf)
- Apps and calendars help, but they are "ineffective when predictions of future menstrual cycles are inaccurate". Designs exclude gender and sexual minorities and fail to support life stages such as young adulthood, pregnancy and menopause. Press coverage called apps "too pink" — [UW News](https://washington.edu/news/2017/05/02/period-tracking-apps-failing-users-in-basic-ways-study-finds); [ICT&health](https://www.icthealth.org/news/apps-tracking-menstrual-cycle-lack-functionality-and-are-too-pink)
- Bull 2019 authors argue for an individualised fertile-window approach rather than population averages — [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2019/aug/individualised-approach-fertile-windows-could-benefit-many-women)

### Inferences
- Valuable: accurate predictions shown with ranges; clear "normal vs see a GP" flags based on NHS/FIGO thresholds; a one-tap summary to share with a GP; support for changing life stages (TTC, postpartum, perimenopause); personal symptom patterns confirmed over several cycles.
- Noise: false-precision ovulation days, generic phase-based lifestyle tips (cycle syncing), and daily nudges with no actionable consequence.
- Privacy (relevant since 2022 in the US, and to UK users generally) is a known user concern, but I did not research it in scope here.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve newer HCI work (for example later CSCW/CHI papers on menopause tracking and data-sharing with clinicians, or Fox and Epstein's work), so evidence on user value after 2017 is thin in these notes.
- I found no quantitative UX data on which specific insight types users rate most valuable. The ranking above is an inference from motivations plus guideline actionability.
