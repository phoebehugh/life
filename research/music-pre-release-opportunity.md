# Pre-release music discovery: is there an opportunity?

Research memo — September 2026

---

## Short answer

The instinct is right, but the obvious version of it is already crowded and the incumbent
just moved. Track ID as a standalone product is a feature war that Beatport entered in
May 2026. "Better Soulseek" is a piracy business. "Direct-to-fan pre-release" has a
well-funded winner in EVEN, backed by Universal.

What is genuinely unowned is narrower and more interesting: **the moment of identification
currently dead-ends commercially.** A fan hears an unreleased track at 2am, wants it, and
there is nothing to buy — no pre-order, no way to pay the producer, no signal sent back to
anyone. Beatport's Track ID deliberately cannot answer for unreleased music because it only
matches its own catalogue. Everyone else answers "it's unreleased" and stops there.

The asset worth owning is not the recogniser — fingerprinting is a commodity (ACRCloud,
Seeqnc, AudD). It's an **opt-in registry of unreleased music plus the demand data that
comes off the dancefloor**: which unsigned track got identified 400 times across 12 cities
before any label signed it. That is a two-sided thing nobody currently has, it is the
currency A&R actually runs on, and it is the one piece that compounds.

---

## What's actually true about the market

- Global electronic music industry: **$15.1bn in 2025**, up 7% from $14.2bn. Recorded music
  +9%, publishing +11%. Germany is the largest market; tech house is Beatport's top-selling
  genre. ([IMS Business Report 2026](https://djmag.com/news/global-electronic-music-industry-value-hits-record-151-billion-ims-business-report-reveals))
- Beatport is the healthy anomaly in downloads: while downloads collapsed globally, Beatport's
  grew **35% over five years**; ~25.5m downloads in 2022, ~12% of all tracks downloaded
  worldwide. Average track $1.29, Beatport takes roughly 50%, subscriptions are ~20% of
  revenue. A Beatport sale is worth **200–500x a stream**. ([Billboard](https://www.billboard.com/pro/beatport-streaming-download-numers-dj-platform/))
- 1001Tracklists had **7.6m users and 68.5m visits in 2023** — a decent proxy for how big
  the track-ID appetite is, and it is still largely manual/human-curated.

So: the money in this genre really does sit at the point of purchase by DJs, not streams.
That part of the thesis holds up well.

## The landscape, honestly

### Track ID — crowded, and the incumbent has arrived

| Who | What | Handles unreleased? |
|---|---|---|
| **Beatport Track ID** (May 2026, built with Seeqnc) | Free in the Beatport app, handles pitch-shift, time-stretch, club noise, blends. Identified tracks land in your history → preview, playlist, buy. | **No** — matches Beatport's released catalogue only |
| **trakd** | Fingerprints unreleased audio directly; gold badge + "First Finder" credit; gamified crowd contributions; imports sets from YouTube/SoundCloud/Mixcloud | Claims yes |
| **setlist.id** | Paste a set URL, ACRCloud against 100m+ tracks, timestamps (by Skylight Studio) | No |
| **set79 / tracksniff / CrateWire / trackradar.ai / djeventsearch** | Variations on URL-in, tracklist-out | No |
| **1001Tracklists / MixesDB / Reddit / Discord** | Human crowdsourcing, the actual default today | Yes, socially |

Read: at least six thin-wrapper products launched into this in ~18 months, all sitting on
the same two or three fingerprinting APIs, and then the company that owns the store, the
catalogue and the DJ relationship shipped it for free. **Recognition is not the business.**

The one unclaimed square in that table is the unreleased column — and note that Beatport
is structurally awkward there, because its whole model requires a track to come through a
label or distributor before it exists in the system.

### Getting music — Beatport, Bandcamp, Soulseek

- **Bandcamp**: still the best artist economics (~82% to artist, ~93% on Bandcamp Fridays)
  but under Songtradr it has had repeated cuts — 50% of staff in 2023, ten more engineers in
  June 2026. Not dying, but no longer a platform anyone wants to be structurally dependent on.
- **Subvert**: the co-op response — announced July 2024, alpha Nov 2025, launched with
  **22,742 member-owners including 8,500+ musicians and 1,500 labels (Warp, Thrill Jockey,
  Polyvinyl)**. Supporter membership is a one-time $100. Worth watching closely; it is the
  most credible values-aligned distribution alternative.
- **SoundCloud** is quietly the most aggressive mover: First Fans (auto-pushes every upload to
  100 taste-matched listeners; 7m+ recommendations delivered), a **US beta from 26 August 2026
  letting selected artists sell downloads direct from their profile**, and it **acquired the
  corpse of Nina Protocol in July 2026**. SoundCloud is walking straight at Bandcamp/Beatport.
- **Soulseek**: still the deep-catalogue tool of choice, no published user numbers, and — being
  blunt — a legal version of it is just Bandcamp. The genuinely unreplicated thing about
  Soulseek is *social*: you can browse a stranger's entire collection. Nobody has rebuilt
  that mechanic legitimately. That's a feature worth stealing, not a company.

### Pre-release / direct-to-fan — largely won, outside of dance

**EVEN** is the closest thing to the idea as stated, and it is a long way down the road:
launched April 2024, **500k+ artists, 3,000+ labels and distributors, 110+ countries, 20%
transaction fee, average fan spend ~$20 to own a release before it hits Spotify**, reports
to Luminate for chart eligibility. J. Cole and Interscope have run campaigns on it, and in
**February 2026 it signed a multi-year deal with Universal Music Group** to power D2C for
UMG artists worldwide.

Smaller entrants (Insidr Music, iKonX) are chasing the unsigned tier with better splits but
no distribution advantage.

What EVEN does *not* serve: underground electronic, where the "release" is a 200-copy 12"
on a label nobody's heard of, the pre-release window is a DJ playing a promo six months
early, and the fan's buying intent is triggered on a dancefloor rather than by an artist's
Instagram.

### Promo pools — B2B, label-priced, fan-invisible

Inflyte (since 2014), FATdrop, Promoly, PromoLink, Promo-Chats, TYFRA. Inflyte is
**£44/mo for 2 promos, £99/mo for 5, £220/mo for 15, or £33 pay-as-you-go**; FATdrop runs
to ~£200/mo; Promoly starts ~£10/mo. Inflyte's differentiator is **RADAR**, which monitors
radio, streams and DJ sets to tell a label who is playing their track.

Two things follow. First, **this tier is priced for labels, not for unsigned producers** —
the people the thesis is about literally cannot afford in. Second, **RADAR proves labels
will pay for "who played my track" data** — but it watches radio and streams, not the
club floor.

## The graveyard, and what it teaches

This is the part I'd weight most heavily, because the failures here are unusually instructive.

- **Aslice** (DVS1, 2022–2024): let DJs voluntarily share a slice of their fee with the
  producers they played. Morally unanswerable, paid out **$400,000+**, and died because
  *"many of the highest paid DJs in the world decided not to contribute."*
  → **Never build a business whose revenue depends on powerful people volunteering money.**
- **Nina Protocol**: shut down May 2026 after **~$50k of lifetime spend across the whole
  platform**, with **77% of releases never collected at all**. Acquired by SoundCloud.
  → **A new destination with better economics does not create demand.** You must attach to
  a moment where demand already exists.
- **Audius**: operational, token down ~95%.
- **Superfan subscriptions**: Vault, Patreon and Spotify have each retreated from the model
  they were meant to prove. Hypebot's framing is "the superfan subscription bubble has burst."
  → **Small artists cannot sustain recurring subs.** One-off transactions at the moment of
  peak desire (EVEN's $20 average) work far better than £5/month forever.
- **Dubset** ($4m, 2017): tried to license DJ mixes for streaming. Dead. Mixcloud solved the
  same problem by doing deals with majors and 250+ indies plus in-house content ID — a decade
  of licensing grind, not a clever product.
  → **Anything requiring master licences at scale is a 5-year business, not a 5-month one.**

There is also a real cultural headwind: a live argument in the scene about whether track ID
tools are *good*. Unreleased tracks, secret edits and unfindable IDs are part of how DJs
build mystique, and plenty of them actively want the information suppressed. Any product here
has to pick a side.

The way through that is to note the **producer's interest is not the DJ's interest**. A DJ
may want the ID hidden; the producer who made it almost always wants credit and money.
Build for the producer, make it opt-in, and the cultural objection largely dissolves.

## Where I think the actual opportunity is

### 1. The pre-release registry — convert the ID moment into a transaction *(strongest)*

A producer or label deposits an unreleased track: audio fingerprint, metadata, release plan,
and a price for early access. When a fan identifies it in a club, instead of a dead end they
get: *"Unreleased — 'Untitled' by X. Out 14 Nov on Y. Pre-order now, or buy the pre-release
for £3."* The producer gets paid at the point of maximum desire, which for this genre is
2am on a dancefloor, not release day.

Two deliberate design choices matter:

- **Store fingerprints, not distributable audio.** Labels are paranoid about pre-release
  leaks — per-recipient watermarking exists precisely because *"it only takes one DJ or
  journalist on the list to leak the track."* A registry that never holds a playable file
  removes the objection and becomes a selling point rather than a risk.
- **Money flows fan → producer,** never DJ → producer. That's the Aslice lesson.

### 2. The demand data is the real asset *(this is the defensible bit)*

Every identification is a signal: track, city, venue, timestamp, and often which DJ. Aggregate
that and you have something nobody has — **dancefloor demand data on music that isn't released
yet.** That is precisely the evidence A&R runs on, and Inflyte has already proved labels pay
for a weaker version of it (radio and streams, via RADAR).

"This unsigned track was IDed 412 times across 12 cities in six weeks" is a fundable A&R
product, a producer's leverage in a deal negotiation, and a moat — because it only exists if
you have both the registry and the fan-side app, and it compounds with every scan.

### 3. A promo lane for the pre-label tier *(natural second act)*

Promo pools start at £33 a drop and assume you have a label. LabelRadar/Beatport Greenroom
routes demos *to* labels. Nobody serves the actual modern path, which is **unsigned producer →
into a respected DJ's crate → demand proves itself → deal**. Layer (2) on top and you can
rank submissions by evidence rather than by who A&R already knows. Note Beatport owns this
funnel today: LabelRadar handles **50,000+ submissions a month across 1,200+ labels**, and
Greenroom launched June 2026 as the artist/label dashboard. Competing head-on here is unwise;
arriving with data they don't have is not.

### Things I'd rule out
- **A standalone track-ID app.** Six exist, Beatport's is free, and the tech is a commodity API.
- **A legal Soulseek.** The demand is for free files and rarities. Steal the collection-browsing
  mechanic; don't rebuild the network.
- **Dubplate/exclusive-drop tooling** as a company. It's real — there's a live market, artists
  sell "dubpacks" of unreleased tracks on Bandcamp and Gumroad in editions of 100, and Fabio
  and Grooverider once spent £2,500–3,000 a month cutting dubplates — but it's a feature.
- **Anything needing master licences up front.** See Dubset.

## The honest risks

1. **Beatport eats it.** They own the store, the catalogue, LabelRadar, Greenroom, Beatport
   Tickets, and now Track ID. Adding "register your unreleased track" is an obvious next step.
   The counter is that Beatport is structurally label-and-catalogue-first and unsigned/unreleased
   sits awkwardly outside that — but realistically this shapes the exit more than the strategy.
2. **Two-sided cold start with two low-willingness-to-pay sides.** Producers won't register until
   fans scan; fans won't scan until there's something to find. Nina died of exactly this.
   The likely unlock is seeding through labels and promo pools who already hold the unreleased
   audio, rather than recruiting producers one at a time.
3. **Small absolute market.** $15.1bn sounds large, but recorded music is a slice of it and
   pre-release is a slice of that. This is plausibly a good £5–20m revenue business, not a
   venture-scale one — which matters a lot for how you'd want to fund it.
4. **Cultural resistance** from DJs who want IDs hidden, and from a scene with sharp instincts
   about extraction. Opt-in and producer-first is the only defensible posture.

---

## What I'd validate next

1. **20 producers with tracks currently being played but unreleased.** Would you register
   fingerprint-only? What would you charge a fan for early access? Would you rather have the
   £3 or the mystique?
2. **10 labels, priced against Inflyte's £99/mo.** Would you pay for club-floor ID demand data?
   This is the fastest test of whether (2) is a real product.
3. **Size the dead end.** Count unanswered "ID?" requests per week across 1001Tracklists,
   r/DJs, trakd and the big Discords. That's your demand proxy and it's countable this week.
4. **Check whether Beatport's Seeqnc arrangement is exclusive**, and what trakd's rights basis
   is for holding unreleased fingerprints — if it's scraped rather than deposited, that's the
   gap to walk through.
5. **Legal read** on an opt-in fingerprint registry and leak liability before anything is built.
