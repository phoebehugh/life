# Predict periods honestly, flag problems clearly, tap once

A period-date-only app can realistically predict your next period to within roughly **two to three days** if your cycles are regular. It cannot pinpoint ovulation, because most of the cycle's natural wobble happens *before* ovulation, in the follicular phase. The figure that matters most for an app builder comes from the largest real-world dataset, **612,613 cycles from 124,648 Natural Cycles users**: the average cycle is **29.3 days, not 28**, the post-ovulation (luteal) phase averages **12.4 days, not 14**, and the pre-ovulation (follicular) phase ranges from about **10 to 30 days** ([Bull et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-019-0152-7)). Even for a perfectly regular 28-day cycle, the best possible single-day guess at ovulation (day 16) is right only about **one time in five** (21%) ([Johnson et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29749274/)). Precise ovulation timing needs a body signal: urine LH tests, temperature, or hormone monitors. The most useful thing a simple tracker can offer is therefore not a confident "ovulation day". It is an honest prediction range, plus clear "worth seeing a GP" flags drawn from FIGO, NHS and NICE thresholds. Those flags cover cycles that are too short, too long or too irregular, periods lasting more than about 8 days, heavy bleeding that affects daily life, bleeding between periods, and perimenopause-style changes. All of them can be worked out from period dates, daily flow and an optional spotting log. On design, the evidence points to three things: make logging trivially easy, forgive gaps, and **avoid daily streaks**, which would break every month by design and are shown to push people to drop off. One caveat applies to the whole report. The first draft relied heavily on search-result snippets because the research proxy blocked most journal and NHS/NICE pages. **On 6 October 2026 the key figures were re-checked against primary sources** (PubMed abstracts, open-access full texts on PMC/Europe PMC, nhs.uk, nice.org.uk, developer.apple.com and developer.android.com). Most held up; a few were corrected or removed, and NICE's fertility guideline CG156 turned out to have been replaced by NG257 in March 2026. Some paywalled full texts (Johnson 2018, Setton 2016, the FIGO 2018 paper itself, the NEJM day-by-day table) still could not be opened, and claims that rest on them stay flagged. The verification log at the end lists what changed.

The earlier report in this series, *Period tracking apps compared*, covered which existing apps to use (Clue, Flo, Apple Health, Natural Cycles, drip, Euki and others), what they cost and how they handle privacy. This report goes underneath: the biology, how prediction works, and what that means for building your own app.

## The 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14 describes a minority of women

The cycle runs on a feedback loop between the brain and the ovaries. In the first half, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) recruits follicles in the ovary. The leading follicle produces rising oestrogen, which eventually flips to *positive* feedback and triggers a surge of luteinising hormone (LH). The surge lasts about 24–48 hours and causes ovulation ([Endotext](https://www.endotext.org/?p=22242); [advancestudy.org](https://advancestudy.org/?p=10545)). The empty follicle then becomes the corpus luteum, which makes progesterone through the second half of the cycle. If no pregnancy occurs, progesterone falls and the period starts. In plain terms, ovulation is the hinge of the cycle. The period is simply the reset that follows about 12 days later.

The textbook version says both halves last 14 days. Large datasets disagree. Bull and colleagues found a mean follicular phase of **16.9 days (95% range 10–30)** and a mean luteal phase of **12.4 days (95% range 7–17)** ([Bull et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-019-0152-7)). A much smaller study of **141 women and 1,060 cycles** using a urine fertility monitor got almost identical averages: follicular 16.5 days (SD 3.4) and luteal 12.4 days (SD 2.0). It concluded that "the follicular phase contributes most to this variability" ([Fehring, Schneider & Raviele 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16700687/)). That study also found that **42.5% of regularly cycling women varied by more than 7 days** from one cycle to the next, and only 25% had all six fertile days between days 10 and 17. (The sample size, 42.5% and 25% figures are in the PubMed abstract; the phase means come from the full text as reported in summaries and were not re-checked.) Two very different methods converging on 12.4 days is the most robust finding in these notes. It means an app that places ovulation at "next period minus 14" puts it on average about a day and a half too early, and several days out in many cycles.

How common is a true 28-day cycle? In a Flo dataset of 1.58 million users, **16.3% had a median cycle of exactly 28 days** ([Grieger & Norman 2020, JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2020/6/e17109/PDF)). In Bull's data, **13% of cycles (81,605 of 612,613) were exactly 28 days**, and even those had an average luteal phase of 12.6 days rather than 14 ([Bull et al. 2019, full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710244/)). An LH-test study of 949 women found that 34% believed they had a 28-day cycle but only 15% did, and that for a 28-day cycle the most likely ovulation day was **day 16, at just 21%** ([Johnson et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29749274/)). (An earlier draft quoted a day 15/16/14 split of 27%/21%/20% attributed to Bull. It is not in the paper, the Natural Cycles/UCL press release or the Oura blog it was traced to, so it has been removed.) In a study of 221 women (696 cycles) with ovulation dated by urine hormones, only about **30% had their fertile window entirely within days 10–17**, and on every day from day 6 to day 21 at least 10% of women were in their fertile window ([Wilcox, Dunson & Baird 2000, BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11082086/)). The authors' conclusion is the one to build an app around: the fertile window "can be highly unpredictable, even if their cycles are usually regular."

### Cycles change predictably across life

Age is the strongest personal factor. Cycles shorten by about **0.18 days per year from age 25 to 45**, almost entirely because the follicular phase gets shorter ([Bull et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-019-0152-7)). The Apple Women's Health Study (165,668 cycles from 12,608 US participants) found that cycle variability is lowest at **ages 35–39**. It is about **45–46% higher under 20 and at 45–49**, and about **200% higher over 50** ([Li et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37248288/); [Harvard summary](https://hsph.harvard.edu/research/apple-womens-health-study/study-updates/menstrual-cycles-today-how-menstrual-cycles-vary-by-age-weight-race-and-ethnicity)). The same study found smaller shifts by ethnicity (Asian participants' cycles averaged 1.6 days longer than white participants') and by very high BMI (about 1.5 days longer at BMI 40 or over). Teenagers' cycles are long and often lack ovulation for the first few years after the first period. ACOG puts adolescent cycles at typically 21–45 days (90% of cycles in the early years), with 60–80% of cycles falling in the adult-like 21–34 day range by the third year after the first period ([ACOG Committee Opinion 651](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2015/12/menstruation-in-girls-and-adolescents-using-the-menstrual-cycle-as-a-vital-sign)).

Several other situations widen the spread. After stopping the pill, a hormonal coil or the ring, Natural Cycles data show a median first cycle of 31 days, back to normal by the second cycle ([Natural Cycles, company source](https://www.naturalcycles.com/research-library/how-soon-does-ovulation-return-after-stopping-hormonal-birth-control)). An older study found disturbances (long cycles, short luteal phases, no ovulation) more common for up to seven cycles, with recovery sometimes taking nine months or more ([Gnoth et al.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/gye.16.4.307.317)). Shift work matters too. In 71,077 nurses, 20 months or more of rotating night shifts raised the risk of irregular cycles by about **23%** (adjusted RR 1.23) ([Lawson et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21364464/)). The evidence on stress comes mainly from small cross-sectional studies and is weaker. In perimenopause, the STRAW+10 staging system defines the **early transition** as a persistent difference of **7 days or more** between consecutive cycle lengths. The **late transition** is marked by **60 days or more without a period** ([STRAW, Fertil Steril](https://www.fertstert.org/article/S0015-0282(01)02909-0/fulltext); [Harlow et al. STRAW+10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3232023/)). The early-transition change begins on average **6–8 years before the final period**, and 12–25% of women see little change at all ([CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/213402)).

### The fertile window is six days, but its position moves

Conception happens only from intercourse in a **six-day window ending on ovulation day**. In the landmark study of 221 women (625 cycles, 192 pregnancies), the chance of conception per act rose from about **10% five days before ovulation to about 33% on ovulation day** ([Wilcox, Weinberg & Baird 1995, NEJM](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7477165/)). The most fertile days are the two to three days before ovulation ([Fertil Steril 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0015028219304327)). There was no meaningful chance of conception the day after ovulation, a point that one open-source period app's issue tracker specifically warns developers about ([lunarlog issue #789](https://github.com/wjdavis5/lunarlog/issues/789)). The full day-by-day probability table circulating online is not in the abstract, and the NEJM full text could not be opened, so it remains **unverified**. Because ovulation day itself shifts by several days from cycle to cycle, the window shown on a calendar has to be wider than six days to be honest.

## Calendar prediction hits a hard ceiling, and body signals break through it

Prediction methods fall into three families. Calendar methods use only period dates. Symptom and temperature methods confirm ovulation in the current cycle. Hormone tests detect the LH surge or oestrogen and progesterone metabolites. The table summarises what each can realistically do.

| Method | What it does | Best evidence on accuracy | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calendar / average of past cycles | Predicts next period and estimates ovulation by counting back | Best single-day ovulation guess on a 28-day cycle (day 16) is right **~21%** of the time, and apps did no better ([Johnson 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29749274/)); the often-quoted ~19% for most apps' pick (day 15) is from the paywalled full text and **unverified** | Only 3 of 33 apps and 1 of 20 websites gave the exact fertile window ([Setton 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27275788/)); 26 of 33 apps included post-ovulation days (press coverage, not in abstract: [BioNews](https://www.progress.org.uk/study-finds-most-fertility-tracker-apps-are-inaccurate/)) |
| Standard Days Method | Avoid days 8–19, only for 26–32 day cycles | 4.75% cumulative pregnancy probability over 13 cycles of correct use, 11.96% typical ([Arevalo 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12057784/)) | Works because the window is wide (12 days), not precise |
| Basal body temperature (BBT) | Confirms ovulation *after* it happens (progesterone raises temperature) | In one study cited by the review, BBT-dated ovulation matched the LH surge ±1 day in only **22% of cycles (17 of 77)** ([Su et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5689497/)) | Not a predictor; NICE says not to use BBT charts to confirm ovulation ([NG257 1.18.9](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng257/chapter/Investigation-of-fertility-problems-and-management-strategies)) |
| Symptothermal (temperature + mucus, strict rules) | Opens and closes fertile phase by double-check rules | **0.6** pregnancies per 100 women over 13 cycles with no unprotected sex in the fertile phase; **1.8** across all users ([Frank-Herrmann 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17314078/)) | Requires training and daily discipline |
| Urine LH tests | Detects the surge before ovulation | Ovulation follows surge onset by **~34 h (range 22–56)** ([Hum Reprod Update 2022 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35258085/)) | ~1–2 days' notice: good for conception, too short for contraception |
| Wearable temperature (Apple Watch, Oura, Ava) | Retrospectively estimates ovulation | Apple (262 participants, 899 cycles): ovulation estimated in **~81%** of cycles with a ≥0.2°C temperature shift, MAE **1.22–1.59 days**; next period MAE **1.65 days**, 89% within ±3 days ([Wang et al., Hum Reprod 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39881571/)) | All validations are maker-sponsored and use LH tests, not ultrasound, as the reference |
| Natural Cycles (temperature + optional LH) | Statistical algorithm, certified as contraception | Pearl Index **1.0 perfect use, 6.8 typical use** (6.9 before truncating at 12 months; 22,785 users) ([Berglund Scherwitzl 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28882680/)); company-authored | US FDA De Novo clearance 2018 ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/fda-grants-natural-cycles-contraception-app-de-novo-marketing-approval)) |

Three lessons come out of this. First, **temperature methods confirm rather than predict**. Any forward-looking fertile window drawn from temperature is still built from past cycles. Second, the well-known effectiveness of the symptothermal method comes from conservative double-check rules, not from precision. Third, once a body signal locates ovulation in the current cycle, period prediction improves sharply, because the remaining 12-or-so-day luteal phase is the stable part. Apple's next-period error of about 1.65 days depends on first detecting ovulation, and its algorithm still produced no ovulation estimate in about a fifth of cycles, even counting only cycles with a clear temperature shift. The study was Apple-funded and used urine LH tests as the reference. Oura reports detecting **96.4%** of ovulations with an average error of about 1.26 days, but that work is company-authored ([Oura](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-ovulation-detection-algorithm-validation-study/)). Consumer hormone monitors such as Inito, Mira and Proov have mostly analytical or manufacturer-sponsored validation, and none was found validated against ultrasound for day of ovulation ([Inito, PMC10247788](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10247788/); [Proov, company blog](https://proovtest.com/blogs/blog/proov-validated)).

### Smarter maths on dates alone helps, but only so far

The most rigorous published work on date-only prediction comes from Columbia University researchers using Clue data from **186,000 users and over 2 million cycles**. Their hierarchical Bayesian model does three things a simple average does not. It pools each user's history with population patterns, so new users get sensible predictions. It updates as the current cycle goes on. And it explicitly models **skipped logging**, recognising that a "60-day cycle" may just be a forgotten period. It beat both summary-statistic and neural-network baselines, and its advantage grew as skipped logging became more likely ([Li, Urteaga et al., arXiv 2102.12439](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12439)). The error figures are sobering, though. At the start of a cycle, its RMSE across all 186,000 users was **7.38 days**, barely better than a plain mean of past cycles (7.50) or median (7.49). The big gain comes later in a long cycle: by day 40, it reached 11.8 days against about 22 for the simple mean, because it recognises probable skipped logs. For users with very consistent cycles, the median absolute error was **1.5 days**, while RMSE stayed high because of occasional missed logs. A companion paper by the same group covers calibrating the uncertainty of these predictions ([Urteaga et al., PMLR 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v149/urteaga21a.html)). A state-space model on athletes with long histories reached an error of about **1.64 days** (RMSE) for cycle length ([Sci Rep 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8379295/)). One non-peer-reviewed analysis put simple models' next-period error at about 6 days ([Malone, Substack](https://maloneperform.substack.com/p/why-menstrual-cycle-predictions-are-less-accurate-than-you-think)). That figure is **low-quality and indicative only**, although it is in the same range as the Clue RMSE above.

Flo claims machine learning cut irregular-cycle prediction error from 5.6 to 2.6 days, using "400+ inputs". This is **company and vendor marketing with no peer review**, and it cannot be compared with Apple's figures because definitions and populations are unknown ([Flo](https://flo.health/flo-accuracy)). The closest published evidence on how much history helps is a robustness check in the Clue paper: its error was roughly flat once at least 4 past cycles per user were available. Statistically, you would expect most of the improvement over the first three to six cycles, as the personal average and spread settle. After that, accuracy is capped by how variable the user's own body is, not by how much data the app holds. Apart from that one check, this is reasoning, not a sourced result.

## The insights worth surfacing are GP flags and personal patterns, not phase tips

Pulling the clinical guidelines together gives a clear ranking. The most valuable insights are those with strong guideline backing that can change what someone does and can be derived from minimal data. Research on menstrual tracking (about 2,000 app reviews, a survey of 687 people and 12 interviews) found that people track mainly **to remember and predict their period** and **to inform conversations with healthcare providers**. Apps become "ineffective when predictions of future menstrual cycles are inaccurate" ([Epstein et al. 2017, CHI](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2017-menstrualtracking.pdf); [UW News](https://washington.edu/news/2017/05/02/period-tracking-apps-failing-users-in-basic-ways-study-finds)). ACOG frames the menstrual cycle as a "vital sign" whose abnormal patterns can point to wider health issues such as PCOS, thyroid disease or bleeding disorders ([ACOG CO 651](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26595586/)).

**Cycle length and regularity flags** need only start dates. FIGO's 2018 definitions for adults treat **24–38 days** as normal frequency, a **shortest-to-longest spread of 7–9 days or less** as regular (9 days at ages 18–25 and 42–45, 7 days at 26–41), and **8 days or less** as normal duration. Flow volume is defined by the patient's own description (light, normal or heavy), not in mL ([Munro et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30198563/); [Jain, Munro & Critchley 2023, FIGO review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952771/); [MSD Manual](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/multimedia/table/normal-menstrual-parameters)). The NHS is looser. It says periods usually come every 21 to 35 days and last 2 to 7 days, and it calls periods irregular if the gap is under 21 or over 35 days ([NHS Periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/periods/); [NHS Irregular periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/irregular-periods/)). Some summaries still cite FIGO's older and looser 2011 regularity definition (up to 20 days' variation), so check that any source uses the 2018 version. For teenagers, ACOG's thresholds are wider: evaluate if periods come more often than every 21 days or less often than every 45, are 90 days apart even once, last more than 7 days, or need a pad or tampon changed every 1–2 hours ([ACOG CO 651](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2015/12/menstruation-in-girls-and-adolescents-using-the-menstrual-cycle-as-a-vital-sign)).

**Heavy bleeding flags** are where daily flow logging earns its keep, and where the UK guidance is distinctive. NICE defines heavy menstrual bleeding not by volume but as "excessive menstrual blood loss which interferes with a woman's physical, social, emotional and/or material quality of life" ([NICE NG88](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Context)). FIGO adopted the same definition. The research definition of over 80 mL per cycle (against an average of 30–40 mL) cannot be measured at home ([GPnotebook](https://gpnotebook.com/en-GB/pages/gynaecology/menorrhagia); [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2007/0615/p1813)). The NHS gives practical markers instead: changing a pad or tampon every **1–2 hours**, using two products at once, periods lasting more than **7 days**, clots bigger than a **10p coin (about 2.5 cm)**, bleeding through to clothes or bedding, avoiding daily activities, and feeling tired or short of breath a lot ([NHS Heavy periods](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/); [NHS 111 Wales](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/heavyperiods,menorrhagia/)). This matters for design. A light/medium/heavy tap is subjective, and "heavy" means different things to different people. The actionable signal is **several consecutive heavy days, a long period, or heavy days that disrupt life**, not the label alone. In **July 2026** NICE removed its 2007 recommendation (1.2.8) against routine serum ferritin (iron) testing in heavy bleeding, saying such a recommendation "is not justified because of the risk of iron deficiency in this population" ([NICE NG88 update information](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng88/chapter/Update-information), last updated 7 July 2026). That supports an "ask your GP about a blood test" prompt for heavy periods with fatigue. Bleeding between periods or after sex is always worth a GP visit ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heavy-periods/)), which is the case for a hidden "spotting" option.

**Perimenopause signals** apply from about age 40. They need roughly ten logged cycles to judge whether a change is "persistent" in the STRAW+10 sense, and the app must not confuse the normal gradual shortening with age for the transition. **Trying-to-conceive support** works best when kept simple. NICE's fertility guideline NG257 (March 2026, replacing CG156) says intercourse every 2 to 3 days optimises the chance of pregnancy ([NG257 1.5.1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng257/chapter/Initial-advice-to-people-concerned-about-delays-in-conception)). Both partners should be offered assessment after **one year** of trying, and anyone aged **36 or over** should be offered specialist referral *at presentation*, without waiting ([NG257 1.16.5 and 1.16.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng257/chapter/Defining-infertility-and-initial-assessment)). NICE does not specify "six months" for older women; that figure comes from other bodies. The NHS says pregnancy tests are most reliable from the first day of a missed period ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/trying-for-a-baby/doing-a-pregnancy-test/)). A neat derived insight: the "day 21" progesterone test is really a mid-luteal test about 7 days before the expected period. NICE itself gives day 21 of a 28-day cycle and day 28 of a 35-day cycle as examples, so the app can tell a 32-day-cycle user to test on day 25 ([NG257 1.18.7–1.18.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng257/chapter/Investigation-of-fertility-problems-and-management-strategies)).

**Symptom patterns** are valuable only when tracked prospectively. PMDD diagnosis under DSM-5 requires at least two cycles of *daily* ratings, because recall is unreliable. Scoring two or more cycles of the standard daily rating form agreed with expert diagnosis about 98% of the time ([Eisenlohr-Moul et al. 2017](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510)). Menstrual migraine is defined as attacks starting between two days before and three days after the period starts, in at least two of three cycles ([ICHD-3](https://ichd-3.org/appendix/a1-migraine/a1-1-migraine-without-aura/a1-1-1-pure-menstrual-migraine-without-aura/)). At the bottom of the ranking sits **"cycle syncing"**: tailoring diet, workouts or work to cycle phases. The best meta-analysis found exercise performance "might be trivially reduced" in the early follicular phase and concluded that "general guidelines on exercise performance across the menstrual cycle cannot be formed" ([McNulty et al. 2020](https://rrmacademy.org/library/the-effects-of-menstrual-cycle-phase-on-exercise-performance-in-eumenorrheic-wom-ouqt6anh/)). Apps that push phase-based lifestyle tips are offering noise dressed as insight.

## One big tap beats every streak, badge and pink gradient

The design evidence lines up with the clinical evidence: do less, do it honestly, and stay out of the way. B.J. Fogg's behaviour model says a behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt coincide. Its key insight is that making a behaviour **easier** is more reliable than raising motivation, and that a small immediate "celebration" helps habits stick ([The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=3058)). Lally's UCL study found habits took on average **66 days** to form (range **18–254**), and that "missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process" ([Lally et al. 2010, Eur J Soc Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674); [University of Surrey interview with Pippa Lally](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)). Self-tracking research treats lapsing and resuming as a normal part of the cycle of use, not a failure ([Epstein et al. 2015, lived informatics](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs5540/au16/readings/PersonalInformatics-Epstein2015.pdf)).

Streaks are the trap. Seven studies by Silverman and Barasch show that highlighting an *intact* streak increases later engagement compared with highlighting a broken one, regardless of how much people actually did. The gap is bigger when people blame themselves for the break and smaller when a streak can be "repaired". The authors frame the cost of broken streaks as "reduced engagement or abandonment" ([Silverman & Barasch 2023, J Consumer Research 49(6)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029); [UD Space](https://udspace.udel.edu/handle/19716/34160); [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)). Period logging happens on only about 3–7 days per cycle, so a daily streak would break every month by design and show users a failure they did not commit. A claim that a 2021 *Computers in Human Behavior* study linked streaks to guilt and abandonment appears only on habit-app blogs. No such paper turned up in a Crossref search of that journal for 2020–2022, so it is **unverified and probably unsupported**. Don't cite it ([yumuuv](https://yumuuv.com/blog/psychology-of-streaks)).

The best models for "one tap and done" come from outside the period category. **Streaks**, an Apple Design Award winner, shows each habit as one big button to tap and hold, and offers many colour themes ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/no/app/id963034692)). **Daylio** logs a mood in two taps, which one review credits with letting people log "even when they are at their worst" ([mHealth review](https://mhealth.amegroups.org/article/view/11509/html)). The benchmark to beat is Apple's own Cycle Tracking, which takes about four taps on the Watch and more on the iPhone ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-mn/guide/watch/apd26429adf0)). Since iOS 17, interactive widgets can run an action without opening the app. iOS 18 control widgets put a button in Control Centre, on the Lock Screen and on the Action button, and a single App Intent can drive all of these plus Siri and Shortcuts ([Apple Developer](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/widgetkit/creating-controls-to-perform-actions-across-the-system.md); [Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/ios-26-widget-and-control-surface)). Zero-tap-to-open logging is therefore technically achievable.

On visual design, the "too pink" criticism is well documented, and privacy-first apps such as drip market their neutral design as a feature ([ICT&health](https://www.icthealth.org/news/apps-tracking-menstrual-cycle-lack-functionality-and-are-too-pink); [dripapp.org](https://dripapp.org/)). Accessibility guidance says not to rely on colour alone. The colour-blind-safe Okabe-Ito palette (for example vermillion #D55E00, blue #0072B2, bluish green #009E73) offers bold colours that stay distinguishable across common colour-vision deficiencies ([ConceptViz](https://conceptviz.app/blog/okabe-ito-palette-hex-codes-complete-reference)). Usefully for syncing, both Apple HealthKit and Android Health Connect model flow as **light / medium / heavy**, plus "unspecified" on Apple (`FLOW_UNKNOWN` on Android) and "none" on Apple only. A three-button UI therefore maps exactly onto both platforms. HealthKit requires the `HKMetadataKeyMenstrualCycleStart` key on every menstrual-flow sample. The old `HKCategoryValueMenstrualFlow` enum is deprecated as of iOS 18, watchOS 11 and macOS 15, replaced by `HKCategoryValueVaginalBleeding` (same five cases: unspecified, light, medium, heavy, none). The `menstrualFlow` sample type itself is not deprecated ([Apple Developer: HKCategoryValueVaginalBleeding](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkcategoryvaluevaginalbleeding); [HKCategoryValueMenstrualFlow](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkcategoryvaluemenstrualflow); [menstrualFlow](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkcategorytypeidentifier/menstrualflow); [Android: MenstruationFlowRecord](https://developer.android.com/reference/kotlin/androidx/health/connect/client/records/MenstruationFlowRecord)). The docs don't show raw values, so whether the two enums share them still needs checking in Xcode. On privacy, Euki's perfect Mozilla score (local storage, no account) and Stardust's 2/10 (an analytics SDK sharing reproductive data) mark the two ends of the spectrum ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/phones/stardust-flo-and-other-popular-period-trackers-flunk-mozillas-latest-privacy-test/)).

## Implications for the app

**Data model.** Store one record per *local calendar day*, not per timestamp. Each record holds `date`, `flow` (light / medium / heavy), and optional `spotting`, `clots`, `flooding` and `affectedMyDay` flags hidden behind a long-press or a single "more" sheet, plus `createdAt` and `updatedAt`. Derive cycle starts rather than storing them: a cycle starts on the first flow day after a gap of several (say 4 or more) flow-free days. Spotting must not start a cycle. The three optional HMB markers and the "affected my day" toggle let the app use the NHS and NICE definitions of heavy bleeding without cluttering the main screen. Optionally store birth year, because age bands drive both priors and flags, and a "life stage" setting (just stopped contraception, trying to conceive, perimenopause) that widens ranges or switches on relevant prompts. The model maps one-to-one to HealthKit daily samples (cycle-start metadata on the first day) and to Health Connect records. Make HealthKit sync opt-in. It is a privacy choice, and it lets Apple's own predictions work if the user prefers them.

**Prediction approach.** Predict the next start as a robust personal average, for example the median or a weighted mean of the last six cycles. Shrink it toward a population prior of about 29 days when fewer than three cycles exist, as the Clue/Columbia model does. Treat any cycle more than about twice the user's median as a probable missed log, not a real long cycle, and ask gently rather than assuming. **Always show a range, never a single date.** Base the range on the user's own spread, and widen it for under-20s, over-45s, the first few cycles after stopping hormonal contraception, and shift workers. If you show a fertile window at all, place estimated ovulation about 12–13 days before the predicted period (not 14), draw six days ending on that day widened by the user's variability, never include days after ovulation, and label it "estimated fertile window — not contraception". A dates-only app should not claim to "predict ovulation". Expect accuracy to plateau after about six cycles. Beyond that, better maths buys little without a body signal.

**Insights worth surfacing.** In rough order of value, these are: the next-period range; a quiet monthly summary (cycle length, period length, number of heavy days); persistence-gated "worth mentioning to your GP" flags; and a one-tap, shareable summary for a GP appointment. Use FIGO 2018 for the flags: cycles under 24 or over 38 days, a spread over 7–9 days by age, and bleeding over 8 days. For missing periods, FIGO 2018 says only "absent", with no day count. Use 90 days as a pragmatic threshold; ACOG uses it for adolescents, and STRAW+10 treats 60 days without a period as late perimenopause. Add NHS heavy-bleeding markers and any spotting between periods. Require two or three cycles of persistence before prompting, to avoid false alarms, and always frame flags as a conversation starter, not a diagnosis. For users over 40, add a gentle STRAW-style "your cycles have become more variable" note after enough cycles. Leave out cycle syncing, daily phase tips and confident ovulation days.

**Design and habit principles.** Put three big buttons on the first screen and the same three in a Home Screen widget, with a Lock Screen or Control Centre control and a Siri phrase driven by one App Intent. Encode flow as a lightness ramp of one warm, non-pink hue, reinforced by one, two or three filled drops so it reads without colour. Confirm with a haptic and a colour fill, not a modal, and let the user change or undo by tapping again. Allow back-filling ("yesterday was medium"). **No daily streaks and no guilt copy.** Frame non-period days as "nothing to log", and if you want a sense of progress, count cycles logged rather than consecutive days. Keep reminders off by default, or limit them to one quiet nudge a day while a period is in progress, stopping automatically after a day or two without logs. Show history as a calm strip of coloured dots per cycle, which doubles as the slowly building "investment" that keeps people coming back. For privacy: local storage, no account, no third-party SDKs, file protection, an optional Face ID lock, export and "delete everything", and a "Data Not Collected" App Store label. Decide deliberately whether the Lock Screen widget should reveal that a period is in progress.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "accuracy" should mean for a simple tracker. The ceiling is set by biology, specifically the 10–30 day spread of the follicular phase, not by algorithmic sophistication. So the winning move for a date-only app is not cleverer prediction but **calibrated honesty**: a range that reflects the user's own variability, and an explicit admission that ovulation timing is a guess. That is also what users say they want, since inaccurate predictions are the main way trust breaks.

The less obvious opportunity is that a three-button flow log, with a couple of hidden markers, already captures almost everything UK clinical guidance cares about: cycle frequency, regularity, duration, heavy bleeding that affects life, and bleeding between periods. That makes a deliberately pared-back app clinically richer than many feature-heavy ones, provided it turns those taps into well-gated, NHS-aligned prompts and a clean summary to take to a GP. Guidance moves, though: NG88 changed in July 2026 and CG156 was replaced by NG257 in March 2026. Re-check the exact wording before shipping.
