# Clue edges Flo as paywalls reshape tracking

For most UK users, **Clue is the best default period tracker**: its free tier still covers core tracking with no ads, it is based in the EU and covered by GDPR, it scores well on independent privacy reviews, and people with irregular cycles or PCOS recommend it most often. **Flo is the most feature-rich and polished app**, but users are angry that its free version keeps shrinking, that it pushes Premium constantly, and that billing and cancellation go wrong. It also carries the heaviest privacy baggage of the big apps. Outside the two market leaders, the right choice depends on what you need. **Apple Health Cycle Tracking** is free, end-to-end encrypted and uses Apple Watch temperature data. **Euki or drip** suit people who want maximum privacy and can live without predictions. **Natural Cycles** is the only app certified as contraception, but it is a paid subscription and is not on the NHS. **Balance**, a free UK app, is the standout for perimenopause. No app predicts irregular cycles reliably, and none of the fertile-window estimates should be treated as contraception unless the app is certified for that. A note on sources: the first draft relied heavily on search-result snippets because direct page fetches were blocked. **On 6 October 2026, primary sources were checked**: the UK App Store listings (prices and ratings), company pages from Flo, Clue, Oura, Natural Cycles and Apple, ftc.gov, legislation.gov.uk, NICE, the King's College London study page, and the class-action counsel's case page. Several figures were corrected as a result; see the verification log at the end. A few sources were still unreachable (Trustpilot, Mozilla Foundation, Reddit, the ICO's own pages, Clue's support site), and claims that rest on them are flagged. App-store prices still vary with promotions and introductory offers, so **confirm the price shown before you tap subscribe**.

## Clue wins on trust, Flo on features, and both now nag for money

The 2026 roundups give the same verdict: "Clue remains the best science-backed tracker, Flo has the most features" ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/the-best-period-tracker-apps-of-2026/)). Bearable makes a competing app, so its ranking is not neutral, but other sources point the same way. Among Reddit recommendations, aggregator data scores **Clue 4.5/5 against Flo's 4.2/5** ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker/)). Flo says it has had **over 500 million downloads** (its own figure, as of July 2026). On the UK App Store it is rated **4.7★ from about 297,000 ratings**, against Clue's **4.7★ from about 110,000** ([UK App Store: Flo](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/flo-period-cycle-health/id1038369065); [UK App Store: Clue](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/clue-period-cycle-tracker/id657189652)). People like logging mood, discharge, sex and pregnancy tests, seeing which cycle phase they are in, and its in-app chat ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker-app/)). Flo also has the only real community feature among the leaders: anonymous "Secret Chats" forums and guided groups ([Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/flo-period-tracker)).

The complaints differ between the two apps. Flo's centre on money. A 2026 review aggregation says "Flo has moved almost everything useful behind a subscription, and the free version now feels empty" ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/is-flo-legit-safe-period-tracker-app-reviews-2026)). Users also report charges after cancelling free trials and renewals they did not authorise. One UK App Store reviewer was **billed £43 as a lump sum after choosing monthly** (a review still visible on the listing in October 2026) ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/flo.health?page=3); [UK App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/flo-period-cycle-health/id1038369065)). An earlier draft gave Flo's Trustpilot rating as 1.4 stars. Current search-index data instead shows a **TrustScore of about 3.9–4.0 from roughly 4,200 reviews, of which about 14% are 1-star**. The Trustpilot page itself could not be opened to confirm this, so treat the figure as unverified. Either way, the 1-star reviews cluster around subscription billing ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/flo.health)). Billing disputes reportedly take **3–5 business days** to get a reply ([19pine.ai](https://www.19pine.ai/customer-service/gym-memberships-and-wellness/how-to-contact-flo-premium-customer-service)).

Clue's complaints centre on design and creeping paywalls. Its late-2022 relaunch removed custom tags, notifications, pregnancy mode and other features, and Clue admitted it had shipped "a slimmer version first". Some features have since come back ([AppitySnacks](https://appitysnacks.com/2022/12/29/when-redesigns-go-wrong-whats-happening-with-clue/); [Clue](https://helloclue.com/articles/about-clue/your-clue-relaunch-questions-answered)). More recently, 2025 reviews describe "at least 2 promotions for Clue Plus every time" the app opens, and say that **notes and cramp levels, once free, now need Plus** ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details/Clue_Period_Tracker_Cycle_Ovulation_Calendar?id=com.clue.android&hl=en_AU)). Both companies are heading the same way. The difference is how far each has gone: Clue's free tier still does the core job, while Flo's is widely seen as hollowed out.

Among the smaller apps, **Stardust** shows how app-store stars can mislead. It is rated **4.8★ from about 13,000 UK ratings** (about 118,000 in the US), but users call its logging "shallow", say it only works for very regular cycles, and resent that **cycle history older than six months is behind a paywall** ([UK App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/stardust-period-tracker/id1495829322); [unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/flo-clue-stardust-apple-health-period-tracking-apps-ranked-2026); [justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1495829322/stardust-period-tracker/reviews)). The six-month history limit comes from review aggregators and was not confirmed on the store page. Simple calendar apps earn praise for fast logging and a clean calendar, but reviewers rarely recommend them. One example is "Period Tracker and Calendar", listed in the UK as "My Calendar – Period Tracker", rated **4.8★ from about 5,800 UK ratings** (about 54,000 in the US) ([iTunes lookup, GB store](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=1032267351&country=gb)).

What you get for the money matters more than the headline price. **Clue Plus** adds the Conceive, Perimenopause and full Pregnancy modes, plus deeper analysis and advanced predictions. ## Predictions work for regular cycles and drift for everything else

There is **no independent, peer-reviewed head-to-head accuracy study of Flo and Clue** that could be verified. Most published numbers come from the companies themselves. Flo says 90% of users report accurate period-start predictions ([Flo](https://flo.health/flo-accuracy)), and its development contractor says a neural network cut average prediction error **from 5.6 to 2.6 days** ([InData Labs](https://indatalabs.com/resources/neural-network-implementation-in-healthcare-app)). Both are marketing claims. Figures circulating for Clue's accuracy come from weak secondary sites and are left out here. The broader research is sobering: a UK survey of tracker users examined how prediction errors, especially for ovulation, affect users' feelings and behaviour ([PMC9047811](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9047811/)).

In practice, users report that both leading apps predict reasonably well once a regular cycle has a few months of data, and that **Flo's algorithm "might struggle with significant cycle inconsistencies"** ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker-app/)). Wearables add real data rather than calendar arithmetic. On Apple Watch Series 8 or later and Ultra, wrist temperature detects the post-ovulation temperature rise and improves period predictions. The ovulation estimate is **retrospective only**, and it needs the watch worn to sleep, with Sleep Focus on, for at least two cycles before it works ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/120357)). Oura's November 2025 update offers 12 months of predictions from a model "designed to be more accurate" for variable cycles ([Oura](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-cycle-insights-update/)). Oura also claims 96.4% ovulation-detection accuracy, but that is company-linked data ([askvora](https://askvora.com/wearables/best-for-womens-health)).

Contraception is a separate category. **Natural Cycles** is a **CE-marked class IIb medical device** and is FDA-cleared. It states that it is **93% effective with typical use and 98% with perfect use**, whether paired with a thermometer, Oura or Apple Watch ([NICE MIB244: regulatory information](https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib244/chapter/Regulatory-information); [Natural Cycles](https://www.naturalcycles.com/); [Natural Cycles on Apple Watch](https://www.naturalcycles.com/research-library/how-effective-is-natural-cycles-when-used-with-apple-watch)). Great Britain currently accepts CE marking for medical devices. Whether Natural Cycles also holds a UKCA mark could not be confirmed. It is **not available on the NHS**. NICE's 2021 medtech innovation briefing (MIB244) is not guidance and makes no recommendation, but the experts it consulted said further evidence was needed before NHS adoption, and it notes that Natural Cycles has not been directly compared with any other contraception ([NICE MIB244](https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib244); [expert comments](https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib244/chapter/Expert-comments)). The UK advertising regulator banned a 2017 advert for presenting the perfect-use figure as if it were typical ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/08/29/contraception-app-natural-cycles-facebook-ad-banned-for-being-misleading)). A 93% typical-use rate is roughly the same as the pill and well below a coil or implant. No other app on this list is designed as contraception, including Flo's and Clue's fertile-window estimates.

## Irregular cycles and perimenopause expose the paywall most sharply

For irregular cycles and PCOS, the community favourite is **Clue's free tier**. An r/PCOS user said it "picked up on irregular periods quickly", and Clue lets you **switch predictions off entirely** so the app does not keep forecasting a period that won't arrive on schedule ([r/PCOS](https://redlib.hbubli.cc/r/PCOS/comments/1j63pl4/period_tracking_apps); [Clue](https://helloclue.com/articles/about-clue/new-in-clue-turning-off-predictions-for-irregular-cycles)). Ovia is sometimes suggested for PCOS, but its fertility scores assume regular cycles and should be ignored ([PCOS Meal Planner](https://app.pcosmealplanner.com/knowledge-articles/any/best-ovulation-tracker-for-pcos-app-reviews-2025)). Stardust struggles with irregular cycles ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/flo-clue-stardust-apple-health-period-tracking-apps-ranked-2026)). If you need to know whether ovulation is happening at all, a wearable's temperature data gives evidence that no calendar app can.

For perimenopause, Clue charges and Flo's position is less clear. **Clue's Perimenopause mode** adds dedicated tracking options for symptoms such as hot flushes, night sweats and brain fog. Clue confirms that "the mode is a part of Clue Plus" ([Clue](https://helloclue.com/perimenopause)). A secondary source dates the mode to 2023 and says it has 14 specific options, including HRT and vaginal dryness; that detail was not confirmed on Clue's site ([FutureFemHealth](https://www.futurefemhealth.com/p/clue-enters-perimenopause-424m-for)). **Flo for Perimenopause** adds a Perimenopause Score, a predicted window for the next period instead of a single date, symptom tracking, perimenopause milestones and daily tips. Flo announced it on 17 July 2025 and said it would be "**available to all users starting July 21, 2025**" ([Flo newsroom](https://flo.health/newsroom/flo-for-perimenopause-is-launching-to-empower-the-1-billion-women-who-experience-perimenopause-without-the-support-they-deserve)). The earlier draft said it reached Flo Premium users in May 2026; that is not supported by Flo's own announcement. Flo's pages do not say which perimenopause features, if any, need Premium, so check in the app. The strongest UK option is **Balance**, from Dr Louise Newson. It is free, rated **4.8★ from about 27,000 UK ratings**, an Apple Editors' Choice, and described on its listing as "**ORCHA reviewed and highly rated**". It tracks symptoms and periods and produces a **Health Report to take to your GP** ([UK App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/balance-hormones-menopause/id1503345959)). The earlier draft's wording, "ORCHA-certified for NHS digital health libraries", was not confirmed. Oura's Perimenopause Check-In produces a shareable PDF score ([The Flow Space](https://www.theflowspace.com/physical-health/prevention-longevity/midlife-wellness-wearables-oura-apple-samsung-3007689/)). A 2026 critique notes, though, that for wearables "the sensors exist, the trials don't" when it comes to perimenopause ([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/08/31/menopause-wearables-sensors-no-trial/)). A cheap, sensible setup for an iPhone user is Apple Cycle Tracking for periods plus Balance for symptoms and GP conversations.

## Privacy splits into local-only apps, EU apps and apps with enforcement histories

Mozilla's "Nothing Personal" testing combined hands-on use with network-traffic analysis. It scored **Euki 10/10, Clue 8, Flo 7, Period Calendar 6, Spot On 5 and Stardust 2** ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/de/nothing-personal/period-ovulation-trackers/); [CyberInsider, 17 July 2026](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/)). These scores were confirmed in a full read of CyberInsider's write-up. Mozilla's own page was still blocked on 6 October 2026. Mozilla also found that turning off Flo's optional ad and analytics settings sharply reduced traffic to partners such as AppsFlyer, but that AppsFlyer had already received a device identifier before Anonymous Mode was switched on. Euki and drip store everything on the phone with no account, which explains their top scores. The trade-off is that Euki is "not the most intelligent", with no predictions or reminders ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/best-period-tracker-apps/)). **Apple Health** is the best compromise. Predictions are calculated on the device. Apple lists Health data as **end-to-end encrypted even under standard data protection**, so you do not need Advanced Data Protection; end-to-end encryption does require two-factor authentication on your Apple Account. Apple cannot read the data ([Apple Support: iCloud data security overview](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/102651); [Apple](https://apple.com.cn/health/pdf/Health_Privacy_Overview_May_2023.pdf)). Clue applies GDPR to all users and said it would refuse US authorities' requests for user data ([Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/news/period-tracking-apps-respond-to-roe-v-wade-ruling)).

Flo's record is the main reason privacy-minded users leave it. In a complaint announced in January 2021, the FTC alleged that Flo had **shared health data from millions of users with marketing and analytics firms, including Facebook and Google, despite promising to keep it private**. The settlement, finalised on 22 June 2021, requires Flo to get users' affirmative consent before sharing health data, commission an independent review of its privacy practices, notify affected users, tell third parties to destroy the data, and stop misrepresenting its data practices. It imposed no fine ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google)). In the US class action that followed (*Frasco v. Flo Health*, N.D. Cal.), the defendants settled for **$59.5M in total**: Google $48M, Flo $8M and Flurry $3.5M ([HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/flo-health-google-flurry-59-5m-settlement-privacy-lawsuit/); [Labaton, class counsel](https://www.labaton.com/cases/frasco-v-flo-health-inc)). On **1 August 2025** a jury found Meta liable for its role in collecting Flo users' data ([Labaton](https://www.labaton.com/cases/frasco-v-flo-health-inc); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/jury-finds-meta-liable-collecting-private-reproductive-health-data)). Plaintiffs have asked for a partial final judgment of about **$1.1B**, covering roughly 222,000 California residents. Meta opposed it in late September 2026, calling it "Frankenstein's monster" ([Law360, 30 Sept 2026](https://www.law360.com/consumerprotection/articles/2531657)). The court hearing set for **29 October 2026** is the final approval (fairness) hearing for the $59.5M settlements with Flo, Google and Flurry ([Labaton](https://www.labaton.com/cases/frasco-v-flo-health-inc)). No date for a ruling on the Meta judgment was confirmed. All of this concerns data shared between 2016 and 2019. Since then Flo has added an **Anonymous Mode**, routed through Cloudflare so that Flo cannot link your data to your identity. It has to be switched on, and it disables some features ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/case-studies/flo-health); [Flo](https://flo.health/product-tour/anonymous-mode)). After Roe was overturned in June 2022, Stardust marketed itself on TikTok as unable to hand over period data if subpoenaed and claimed end-to-end encryption. TechCrunch found that it did not offer true end-to-end encryption and that it shared phone-login users' details with the analytics firm Mixpanel. Stardust later removed the encryption claim from its privacy policy ([Truth in Advertising](https://truthinadvertising.org/blog/period-tracker-capitalizes-on-post-roe-fears/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/)).

The UK context lowers the stakes but does not remove them. The ICO's review (announced September 2023, concluded February 2024) "did not flag serious compliance issues". It still reminded developers to be transparent, obtain valid consent, use a correct lawful basis and be accountable ([LexisNexis, 8 Feb 2024](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/news/ico-advises-app-developers-on-protecting-users-privacy-following-review)). The ICO's September 2023 polling found that **over half of app users believed they had seen more baby or fertility ads since signing up**, and 17% found those ads distressing ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2023/09/ico-to-review-period-and-fertility-tracking-apps)). The ICO page was not reachable on 6 October 2026, so the poll figures are confirmed only by the search index and contemporaneous press coverage. A King's College London and UCL study, presented at the CHI 2024 conference, analysed **20 popular female-health apps** on the UK and US Google Play stores, including Flo and Clue. It found that **35% claimed no third-party sharing in their Play Store data-safety labels while describing some sharing in their privacy policies**. Some apps also required users to state whether they had miscarried or had an abortion, and some made data hard to delete ([KCL](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/female-health-apps-misuse-highly-sensitive-data-study-finds); [UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/may/female-health-apps-misuse-highly-sensitive-data)). The legal risk largely disappeared in England and Wales with **section 241 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026**. It provides that "no offence is committed by a woman acting in relation to her own pregnancy" under abortion law, and it came into force at Royal Assent on **29 April 2026** ([legislation.gov.uk, s.241](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/20/section/241); [s.255](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/20/section/255)). That followed 2025 police guidance allowing officers to examine period-app data after a pregnancy loss ([Humanists UK](https://humanists.uk/2026/04/29/success-decriminalisation-of-abortion-and-historic-pardons-for-women-become-law/); [Humanists UK 2025](https://humanists.uk/2025/06/04/police-access-to-period-apps-highlights-need-to-decriminalise-abortion/)). For a UK user, the remaining risk is commercial (ad targeting, SDK leakage, breaches) rather than legal.

## Conclusion

The market has flipped since the early days, when the free apps competed on features. In 2026 the leading apps compete on how much they hold back. Paywalls now cover exactly the groups with the most complex needs: people trying to conceive, pregnant users, and people in perimenopause. Meanwhile, the best free experiences come from Apple, from UK clinical apps like Balance, and from open-source local apps that have no reason to monetise your data. A sensible strategy is to keep your core log in a free, privacy-respecting tool and pay only for a specific mode when you need it, choosing annual billing knowingly.

Two uncertainties remain. It is unclear which parts of Flo's perimenopause suite (launched July 2025) are free and which need Premium, and little independent user feedback on it has surfaced. The court has also yet to rule on the proposed $1.1B judgment against Meta, which could change how app developers think about sharing data with ad platforms. The 29 October 2026 hearing is the final approval of the separate $59.5M settlements with Flo, Google and Flurry. The UK prices above were checked on the App Store on 6 October 2026, but promotional tiers change often, so confirm the price on the purchase screen before committing to a subscription.
