# Clue edges Flo as paywalls reshape tracking

For most UK users, **Clue is the best default period tracker**: its free tier still covers core tracking with no ads, it is based in the EU and covered by GDPR, it scores well on independent privacy reviews, and people with irregular cycles or PCOS recommend it most often. **Flo is the most feature-rich and polished app**, but users are angry that its free version keeps shrinking, that it pushes Premium constantly, and that billing and cancellation go wrong. It also carries the heaviest privacy baggage of the big apps. Outside the two market leaders, the right choice depends on what you need. **Apple Health Cycle Tracking** is free, end-to-end encrypted and uses Apple Watch temperature data. **Euki or drip** suit people who want maximum privacy and can live without predictions. **Natural Cycles** is the only app certified as contraception, but it is a paid subscription and is not on the NHS. **Balance**, a free UK app, is the standout for perimenopause. No app predicts irregular cycles reliably, and none of the fertile-window estimates should be treated as contraception unless the app is certified for that. One caveat applies throughout: the research environment blocked most direct page fetches (app stores, Reddit, Clue's support site, Mozilla), so **much of the evidence below comes from search-result snippets rather than full pages**. Star ratings, review counts and especially **UK prices should be checked in the app before you subscribe**.

## Clue wins on trust, Flo on features, and both now nag for money

The 2026 roundups give the same verdict: "Clue remains the best science-backed tracker, Flo has the most features" ([Bearable](https://bearable.app/the-best-period-tracker-apps-of-2026/)). Bearable makes a competing app, so its ranking is not neutral, but other sources point the same way. Among Reddit recommendations, aggregator data scores **Clue 4.5/5 against Flo's 4.2/5** ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker/)). Flo has about **420 million downloads**, and people like logging mood, discharge, sex and pregnancy tests, seeing which cycle phase they are in, and its in-app chat ([pregnancyapp.com](https://pregnancyapp.com/best-period-app/); [GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker-app/); [UK App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/flo-period-cycle-health/id1038369065)). Flo also has the only real community feature among the leaders: anonymous "Secret Chats" forums and guided groups ([Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/flo-period-tracker)).

The complaints differ between the two apps. Flo's centre on money. A 2026 review aggregation says "Flo has moved almost everything useful behind a subscription, and the free version now feels empty" ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/is-flo-legit-safe-period-tracker-app-reviews-2026)). Users also report charges after cancelling free trials, renewals they did not authorise, and one UK reviewer was **billed £43 as a lump sum after choosing monthly** ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/flo.health?page=3); [UK App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/flo-period-cycle-health/id1038369065)). Flo's Trustpilot rating is about **1.4 stars from 2,000+ reviews**. That figure overstates the problem, because Trustpilot attracts billing complaints and Flo's app-store ratings are much higher, but it shows where the anger is ([Trustpilot](https://au.trustpilot.com/review/flo.health)). Billing disputes reportedly take **3–5 business days** to get a reply ([19pine.ai](https://www.19pine.ai/customer-service/gym-memberships-and-wellness/how-to-contact-flo-premium-customer-service)).

Clue's complaints centre on design and creeping paywalls. Its late-2022 relaunch removed custom tags, notifications, pregnancy mode and other features, and Clue admitted it had shipped "a slimmer version first". Some features have since come back ([AppitySnacks](https://appitysnacks.com/2022/12/29/when-redesigns-go-wrong-whats-happening-with-clue/); [Clue](https://helloclue.com/articles/about-clue/your-clue-relaunch-questions-answered)). More recently, 2025 reviews describe "at least 2 promotions for Clue Plus every time" the app opens, and say that **notes and cramp levels, once free, now need Plus** ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details/Clue_Period_Tracker_Cycle_Ovulation_Calendar?id=com.clue.android&hl=en_AU)). Both companies are heading the same way. The difference is how far each has gone: Clue's free tier still does the core job, while Flo's is widely seen as hollowed out.

Among the smaller apps, **Stardust** shows how app-store stars can mislead. It is rated 4.6★ from 28,000+ ratings, but users call its logging "shallow", say it only works for very regular cycles, and resent that **cycle history older than six months is behind a paywall** ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/flo-clue-stardust-apple-health-period-tracking-apps-ranked-2026); [justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1495829322/stardust-period-tracker/reviews)). Simple calendar apps such as "Period Tracker and Calendar" (4.8★ from about 51,500 ratings) earn praise for fast logging and a clean calendar, but reviewers rarely recommend them ([AppPricingLab](https://apppricinglab.com/app/apple/1032267351)).

## Predictions work for regular cycles and drift for everything else

There is **no independent, peer-reviewed head-to-head accuracy study of Flo and Clue** that could be verified. Most published numbers come from the companies themselves. Flo says 90% of users report accurate period-start predictions ([Flo](https://flo.health/flo-accuracy)), and its development contractor says a neural network cut average prediction error **from 5.6 to 2.6 days** ([InData Labs](https://indatalabs.com/resources/neural-network-implementation-in-healthcare-app)). Both are marketing claims. Figures circulating for Clue's accuracy come from weak secondary sites and are left out here. The broader research is sobering: a UK survey of tracker users examined how prediction errors, especially for ovulation, affect users' feelings and behaviour ([PMC9047811](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9047811/)).

In practice, users report that both leading apps predict reasonably well once a regular cycle has a few months of data, and that **Flo's algorithm "might struggle with significant cycle inconsistencies"** ([GummySearch](https://gummysearch.com/tools/best-products/period-tracker-app/)). Wearables add real data rather than calendar arithmetic. On Apple Watch Series 8 or later and Ultra, wrist temperature detects the post-ovulation temperature rise and improves period predictions. The ovulation estimate is **retrospective only**, and it needs the watch worn to sleep for at least two cycles before it works ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/120357)). Oura's November 2025 update offers 12 months of predictions from a model "designed to be more accurate" for variable cycles ([Oura](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-cycle-insights-update/)). Oura also claims 96.4% ovulation-detection accuracy, but that is company-linked data ([askvora](https://askvora.com/wearables/best-for-womens-health)).

Contraception is a separate category. **Natural Cycles** is CE-marked and FDA-cleared, and claims **93% effectiveness with typical use and 98% with perfect use**, whether paired with a thermometer, Oura or Apple Watch ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/11/09/natural-cycles-gets-30m-for-its-eu-certified-digital-contraception/?ncid=rss); [Natural Cycles](https://www.naturalcycles.com/research-library/how-effective-is-natural-cycles-when-used-with-apple-watch)). It is **not available on the NHS**: NICE concluded that more evidence is needed ([NICE MIB244](https://www.nice.org.uk/advice/mib244/resources/natural-cycles-for-monitoring-fertility-pdf-2285965632946885)). The UK advertising regulator banned a 2017 advert for presenting the perfect-use figure as if it were typical ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/08/29/contraception-app-natural-cycles-facebook-ad-banned-for-being-misleading)). A 93% typical-use rate is roughly the same as the pill and well below a coil or implant. No other app on this list is designed as contraception, including Flo's and Clue's fertile-window estimates.

## Irregular cycles and perimenopause expose the paywall most sharply

For irregular cycles and PCOS, the community favourite is **Clue's free tier**. An r/PCOS user said it "picked up on irregular periods quickly", and Clue lets you **switch predictions off entirely** so the app does not keep forecasting a period that won't arrive on schedule ([r/PCOS](https://redlib.hbubli.cc/r/PCOS/comments/1j63pl4/period_tracking_apps); [Clue](https://helloclue.com/articles/about-clue/new-in-clue-turning-off-predictions-for-irregular-cycles)). Ovia is sometimes suggested for PCOS, but its fertility scores assume regular cycles and should be ignored ([PCOS Meal Planner](https://app.pcosmealplanner.com/knowledge-articles/any/best-ovulation-tracker-for-pcos-app-reviews-2025)). Stardust struggles with irregular cycles ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/flo-clue-stardust-apple-health-period-tracking-apps-ranked-2026)). If you need to know whether ovulation is happening at all, a wearable's temperature data gives evidence that no calendar app can.

For perimenopause, both leaders now charge. **Clue's Perimenopause mode** (since 2023) adds 14 specific tracking options, including hot flushes, night sweats, brain fog, HRT and vaginal dryness, and **requires Clue Plus** ([FutureFemHealth](https://www.futurefemhealth.com/p/clue-enters-perimenopause-424m-for)). **Flo for Perimenopause** has a symptom checker, a severity score, a menopause timeline and a guide to relief options, and has been **available to Flo Premium users since May 2026** ([Flo](https://flo.health/newsroom/flo-for-perimenopause-is-launching-to-empower-the-1-billion-women-who-experience-perimenopause-without-the-support-they-deserve)). No user feedback on Flo's launch has surfaced yet. The strongest UK option is **Balance**, from Dr Louise Newson. It is free, rated about 4.8★ from roughly 27,000 ratings, an Apple Editors' Choice, and **ORCHA-certified for NHS digital health libraries**. It tracks symptoms and periods and produces a **Health Report to take to your GP** ([App Store GB](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/balance-hormones-menopause/id1503345959)). Oura's Perimenopause Check-In produces a shareable PDF score ([The Flow Space](https://www.theflowspace.com/physical-health/prevention-longevity/midlife-wellness-wearables-oura-apple-samsung-3007689/)). A 2026 critique notes, though, that for wearables "the sensors exist, the trials don't" when it comes to perimenopause ([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/08/31/menopause-wearables-sensors-no-trial/)). A cheap, sensible setup for an iPhone user is Apple Cycle Tracking for periods plus Balance for symptoms and GP conversations.

## Privacy splits into local-only apps, EU apps and apps with enforcement histories

Mozilla's "Nothing Personal" testing combined hands-on use with network-traffic analysis. It scored **Euki 10/10, Clue 8, Flo 7, Period Calendar 6, Spot On 5 and Stardust 2** ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/de/nothing-personal/period-ovulation-trackers/); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/)). Euki and drip store everything on the phone with no account, which explains their top scores. The trade-off is that Euki is "not the most intelligent", with no predictions or reminders ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/best-period-tracker-apps/)). **Apple Health** is the best compromise: predictions are calculated on the device, and with two-factor authentication on, iCloud Health sync is end-to-end encrypted so Apple cannot read it ([Apple](https://apple.com.cn/health/pdf/Health_Privacy_Overview_May_2023.pdf)). Clue applies GDPR to all users and said it would refuse US authorities' requests for user data ([Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/news/period-tracking-apps-respond-to-roe-v-wade-ruling)).

Flo's record is the main reason privacy-minded users leave it. In 2021 the FTC found that Flo had **shared health data with Facebook and Google despite promising not to**, but imposed no fine ([FTC](https://data.aclum.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/FTC_www_ftc_gov_news-events_news_press-releases_2021_06_ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google.pdf)). In the US class action that followed, Flo, Google and Flurry settled for **$59.5M in total** ([HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/flo-health-google-flurry-59-5m-settlement-privacy-lawsuit/)). In August 2025 a jury found Meta liable for intercepting Flo users' data ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/jury-finds-meta-liable-collecting-private-reproductive-health-data)), and a proposed judgment of about **$1.1B** was still being fought in late September 2026 ([Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2531657/meta-fights-monster-proposed-1-1b-cipa-judgment)). All of this concerns data shared between 2016 and 2019. Since then Flo has added an **Anonymous Mode**, routed through Cloudflare so that Flo cannot link your data to your identity. It has to be switched on, and it disables some features ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/case-studies/flo-health); [Flo](https://flo.health/product-tour/anonymous-mode)). Stardust's post-Roe claim of end-to-end encryption turned out to be false, and it shared data with analytics firms ([Truth in Advertising](https://truthinadvertising.org/blog/period-tracker-capitalizes-on-post-roe-fears/)).

The UK context lowers the stakes but does not remove them. The ICO's 2023–24 review found "no serious compliance issues", but its polling showed that **over half of users believed they saw more baby or fertility ads after signing up**, and 17% found those ads distressing ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2023/09/ico-to-review-period-and-fertility-tracking-apps); [LexisNexis](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/news/ico-advises-app-developers-on-protecting-users-privacy-following-review)). A KCL/UCL study found that **35% of female-health apps claimed no third-party sharing on their Google Play labels while describing sharing in their privacy policies** ([UCL](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2024/may/female-health-apps-misuse-highly-sensitive-data)). The legal risk largely disappeared in England and Wales when the **Crime and Policing Act 2026** removed women ending their own pregnancies from the criminal law on 29 April 2026. That followed 2025 police guidance allowing officers to examine period-app data after a pregnancy loss ([Humanists UK](https://humanists.uk/2026/04/29/success-decriminalisation-of-abortion-and-historic-pardons-for-women-become-law/); [Humanists UK 2025](https://humanists.uk/2025/06/04/police-access-to-period-apps-highlights-need-to-decriminalise-abortion/)). For a UK user, the remaining risk is commercial (ad targeting, SDK leakage, breaches) rather than legal.

## Quick picks for a UK reader

| If you want… | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A solid free default | **Clue** (free) | No ads, EU/GDPR, can turn off predictions for irregular cycles |
| The most features and community | **Flo** (Premium, with Anonymous Mode on) | Richest logging and content, Secret Chats; watch billing |
| Free, private and low effort on iPhone | **Apple Health Cycle Tracking** | E2E encrypted sync; temperature-based estimates with Watch Series 8+ |
| Maximum privacy | **Euki or drip** | Local-only, no account; no predictions (Euki) |
| Perimenopause | **Balance** (free) + Apple Health or Clue Plus | NHS-library certified, GP report |
| Hormone-free contraception | **Natural Cycles** | The only certified option; paid, not on NHS, 93% typical use |

## Conclusion

The market has flipped since the early days, when the free apps competed on features. In 2026 the leading apps compete on how much they hold back. Paywalls now cover exactly the groups with the most complex needs: people trying to conceive, pregnant users, and people in perimenopause. Meanwhile, the best free experiences come from Apple, from UK clinical apps like Balance, and from open-source local apps that have no reason to monetise your data. A sensible strategy is to keep your core log in a free, privacy-respecting tool and pay only for a specific mode when you need it, choosing annual billing knowingly.

Two uncertainties remain. Flo's perimenopause suite is new and has no track record with users yet, and the outcome of the Meta judgment hearing on 29 October 2026 could change how app developers think about sharing data with ad platforms. Because so much of this evidence came from search snippets rather than full pages, recheck the prices and ratings in the App Store or Google Play before committing to a subscription.
